Popejoy Hall at the University of New Mexico in the 1970s
Popejoy Hall, located on the University of New Mexico (UNM) campus in Albuquerque, became an important center for performing arts in New Mexico during the 1970s. The decade marked a significant period in the development of the university’s cultural life, as Popejoy helped connect students and local residents with professional theater, music, dance, and other live performances. To understand its history, it is helpful to look at the hall’s origins, its role during the 1970s, and its growing importance to Albuquerque’s arts community.
From the 1970s: A growing cultural center
Although Popejoy Hall officially opened on October 1, 1966, its influence continued to grow throughout the 1970s. The inaugural performance featured the Utah Symphony Orchestra, establishing the venue as a place for major professional performances in New Mexico. The hall was originally called the University Concert Hall and was renamed Popejoy Hall on May 5, 1968, in honor of former UNM president Thomas L. Popejoy. He had served as president from 1948 to 1968 and spent much of his career advocating for a professional performing arts venue on campus. According to UNM’s historical account, Popejoy envisioned the hall as a way to connect the university with the surrounding community.
UNM UCAM Newsroom+1
During the 1970s, Popejoy operated in a period of significant change for UNM. The university experienced student activism, political demonstrations, and debates over the Vietnam War and civil rights. On May 4, 1970, protests on campus escalated to the point that New Mexico Governor David Cargo ordered National Guard troops onto university grounds. A photograph preserved by UNM’s Center for Southwest Research shows the troops and demonstrators on campus, with Popejoy Hall visible in the background. Although the incident was not an event held inside the hall, it illustrates the political atmosphere surrounding the university during the early part of the decade.
historicpreservation.unm.edu+1
Popejoy’s central purpose, however, remained the presentation of live performing arts. Its large auditorium gave Albuquerque a venue capable of accommodating major orchestras, touring musicians, theatrical productions, and other professional performers. The hall seated approximately 2,000 people, making it an important resource for both university programming and the broader public. UNM’s 2016 anniversary history explains that the hall was built partly to attract professional performance companies and provide local audiences with cultural experiences that previously required travel outside the city.
UNM UCAM Newsroom+1
Performances and artists in the 1970s
Archived concert listings provide examples of the variety of performances associated with Popejoy during the decade. According to Concert Archives, April 4, 1977, included performances listed for Renaissance and Gallagher & Lyle. On April 17, 1977, listings included folk guitarist Leo Kottke and comedian Martin Mull. Jazz saxophonist Grover Washington Jr. was listed for April 30, 1977, while singer-songwriter Bonnie Raitt appeared on November 20, 1977. These examples suggest that Popejoy’s programming extended beyond traditional orchestral music to include jazz, folk, rock, and comedy.
Concert Archives+1
The variety continued in 1978. Concert Archives lists British rock band 10cc performing on November 5, 1978, and folk musician Arlo Guthrie appearing with Shenandoah on November 15, 1978. On February 25, 1979, the American rock band The Tubes was listed at Popejoy Hall. Together, these entries provide a glimpse of the different musical audiences the venue attracted toward the end of the decade. However, Concert Archives is a secondary concert-history database, so individual dates should ideally be cross-checked against contemporary newspaper advertisements or original university programs.
Concert Archives+1
Lasting significance
By the end of the 1970s, Popejoy Hall had become an established part of Albuquerque’s cultural infrastructure. Its location on the UNM campus allowed university students to experience professional performances while giving residents throughout New Mexico access to touring artists and large-scale productions. The hall also supported the university’s wider commitment to music, theater, and dance education.
Popejoy’s importance during the decade was therefore not simply architectural. It represented the realization of Thomas Popejoy’s long-standing vision of a university that served as a cultural resource for the public. The 1970s helped establish the hall’s identity as a venue for a broad range of performers and audiences, laying groundwork for its continued role in New Mexico’s performing arts community.
